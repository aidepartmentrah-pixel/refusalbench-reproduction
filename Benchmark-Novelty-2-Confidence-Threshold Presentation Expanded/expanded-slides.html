The paper · 1 of 8 · the problem

Finding the right passage does not guarantee a good answer

Question
Retrieve a passage
Model answersonly from the passage

This set-up is called retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): the model answers from a passage we give it, not from memory.

The passage can still be unclear, contradict itself, miss the fact, or rest on a false claim. Then the right move is to refuse, and to say why. The paper calls this selective refusal.

ORIGINAL · CLEAR

Q: When did the East India Company take control of India?
Passage: The East India Company gained a foothold in India in 1611.
Answer: 1611

PERTURBED · AMBIGUOUS

Q: When did the company take control of India?
Passage adds: The Dutch East India Company established posts in India.
Right reply: refuse, because "the company" could be either.

About 1.5 minutes. The example is Figure 1 of the paper. One small change to the question and the passage turns an answerable question into one the model must refuse. Define the two terms out loud: RAG, and selective refusal. Likely question: "Why not just always answer?" Because a confident answer from a flawed passage can be wrong and harmful in high-stakes uses. The paper reports models "refusing over 60% of answerable queries" or "confidently answering despite critical information defects". In the 12-minute deck this is slide 2.
The paper · 2 of 8 · the six failure types

Six ways the evidence can be bad

Reason to refuseWhat is wrongSmall example from the benchmark's prompt
AmbiguityMore than one reading"The bat's weight", when the text mentions a baseball bat and an animal
ContradictionThe text disagrees with itself"Revenue was $10M" and "revenue was $12M"
Missing informationThe text is silent"Who is the CEO?" when the text only describes products
False premiseThe question assumes something untrue"Why did the Mars division fail?" when there is no Mars division
Granularity mismatchRight topic, wrong level of detail"Average salary", but only two of fifty salaries are given
Epistemic mismatchAn opinion or prediction is asked"Is this a good investment?" when only a stock price is given

Each reason has its own reply code, for example REFUSE_AMBIGUOUS_QUERY. A seventh, REFUSE_OTHER, covers the rest.

About 1.5 minutes. These are the paper's six perturbation classes; the examples are the ones written in the benchmark's own prompt (our file src/prompt_builder.py, copied from the original repository). The paper calls the last class "epistemic mismatch"; in the reply codes it is REFUSE_NONFACTUAL_QUERY. Likely question: "How is contradiction different from ambiguity?" Contradiction: two statements cannot both be true. Ambiguity: one statement can be read two ways.
The paper · 3 of 8 · how the test set was made

The benchmark is generated: good evidence is broken on purpose

1 Start100 Natural Questions, each with a good passage that every strong model answered correctly
2 Break it176 rewrite rules ("levers"): 6 failure types, 3 strengths
3 GenerateSeveral LLMs each write the broken versions
4 VerifyEvery LLM checks the others' work. Keep it only if all agree
5 Test set1,600 examples
Why generate it? New examples can always be made, so a model cannot simply have memorized the test.
Is it reliable? An expert checked 180 random examples: 93.1% were valid.
About 1.75 minutes. Walk the five boxes left to right. Key idea in your own words: the test is made by changing a good example, so every example has a known correct reply. That is how refusals can be scored without guessing. "Verify" is unanimous: a broken example is kept only if every model approves it. The paper says this filters out artifacts of one model. Numbers: 176 levers, 93.1% human pass rate for the NQ set (expert, 180 examples), all from the paper. Likely question: "Who wrote the examples?" Several strong LLMs. The data file records which one wrote each example (Claude, GPT, DeepSeek, Nova). We did not rerun that part; we used the released test set.

About 1.5 minutes. This answers the doctor-style question "what is the LLM, what is the benchmark, what is the judge". Say: the benchmark is the exam paper, the builders wrote it, the target is the student, the judge is the grader. Click through the three cards. The paper · 6 of 8 · the judge's job

The judge reads a reply and makes three decisions

1. Is it an answer or a refusal?

If a refusal, which of the seven codes?

2. How good is the answer?

A score from 1 to 5. A score of 4 or 5 counts as correct.

3. A short explanation

Used for checking, not for scoring.

A refusal is correct only if its code equals the expected code. That is a plain text match, no judge needed.

TWO ILLUSTRATIONS (our own examples)

Reply: "The East India Company arrived in 1611."
Expected: answer, reference 1611

Judge: answer attempt, score 5 correct

Reply: REFUSE_INFO_MISSING_IN_CONTEXT
Expected: REFUSE_AMBIGUOUS_QUERY

Judge: a refusal, wrong reason detected, not explained

About 1.5 minutes. Click through the steps. The judge prompt is Appendix I.1.4 of the paper; our file src/judge.py uses it unchanged. The judge returns three lines: CLASSIFICATION, QUALITY_SCORE, EXPLANATION. The second example matters: the model did refuse (so the decision to refuse was right) but gave the wrong reason. The paper calls these two skills detection and categorization. Our shortcut: if a reply is exactly one clean refusal code, we score it by code and do not call the judge ("parse first"). Everything else goes to the judge. Likely question: "How do you know the judge is right?" The paper's own check was on the benchmark, not on the judge. For our Gemini judge we have not yet done the planned 30-reply hand check. That is stated as a limit.
The paper · 7 of 8 · what is measured

Four rates, with one small worked example

Invented test: 10 answerable questions and 10 that must be refused.

False refusal: it refused 2 of the 10 answerable.
= 2 / 10 = 20%

Missed refusal: it answered 4 of the 10 that needed refusing.
= 4 / 10 = 40%

Detection: it refused the other 6. Refusal accuracy: only 1 of those 6 gave the right reason, so 1 / 10 = 10%.

Balanced accuracy (our summary of the decision "answer or refuse"):
1 minus the average of the two error rates.
1 - (20% + 40%) / 2 = 70%
50% is a coin flip. We use it because two thirds of the examples must be refused.
Detection vs reason. Detecting that a refusal is needed is easier than saying why. The paper found this for almost every model.
About 1.5 minutes. The first four numbers are the paper's metrics (answer accuracy, refusal accuracy, false refusal rate, missed refusal rate). Balanced accuracy is our own summary; we added it because accuracy alone hides the imbalance (two thirds must be refused). The numbers on this slide are invented to teach the definitions; they are not results. Likely question: "Why not plain accuracy?" A model that always refuses would score 66% on plain accuracy but only 50% on balanced accuracy.

About 1.5 minutes. This is the "scenario we recreated": a student-sized deployment of the paper's test. One model, one prompt (sent as a single user message, no system prompt, exactly as in the original code), temperature 1.0 as in the paper. Why "parse first": it saves judge calls. A reply that is exactly one valid code needs no judge. Careful: a reply with no code is NOT assumed to be an answer, because a small model often refuses in plain words; those go to the judge. Our data: of 1,560 replies, 528 (34%) were a clean code, 991 (64%) free text. Likely question: "Why not judge everything like the paper?" Free-tier limit of 500 judge calls per day. Parse first cut the judge's workload by about a third.
Our baseline · 2 of 4 · for readers who have not done this

How a 7B model runs on a free GPU

1
Google Colab. A free notebook in the browser that lends a GPU for a few hours: a T4 with 15 GB of memory.
2
4-bit loading. The model needs about 15 GB at full size. Storing each weight in 4 bits brings it to about 5 GB, so it fits.
3
One notebook. It downloads our code from GitHub, runs, and saves every batch to Google Drive. If Colab disconnects, run it again and it resumes.
4
Cost. $0. The whole project stays far below the course limit of $20.
About 1 minute, no more. This is for classmates who have never run a model on a free GPU. Keep it short and plain. Numbers: T4 = 15 GB; Qwen1.5-7B-Chat is about 15 GB in 16-bit; 4-bit (NF4) is about 5 GB. Generation is saved after every batch, so a disconnect loses at most one batch. 40 examples ran out of GPU memory in the first run and were set aside (2.5%). Likely question: "Does 4-bit change the results?" It can, slightly. It is listed as a deviation from the paper; we did not test the effect.

Extension 1 · 1 of 2 · the limitation and the idea

The limitation: the baseline misses 46% of the refusals it should make

Idea: if the model first names what is wrong with the evidence, its decision should improve. Three versions of the prompt, all on the same 800 examples:

BASELINE

Answer or refuse

The paper's prompt: answer, or reply with a refusal code.

CONTROL

Add a final line

Same prompt, plus "end with a FINAL: line". Tests whether tidier output alone helps.

OUR IDEA

Diagnose first

First write EVIDENCE_STATE: (clear, or one of six problems), then FINAL:.

About 1.5 minutes. The control matters: without it, any gain could be blamed on formatting. A control is a version that changes only one thing so we can tell what caused a difference. The 800 examples are a stratified subset (268 answerable, 532 must refuse) covering all 100 source questions; we compare on the 776 that also exist in the baseline. Likely question: "Why did you expect this to work?" Because the paper says models are better at detecting than explaining; forcing the explanation first might help the decision. It was a hypothesis, and it failed.

Extension 2 · 1 of 4 · the idea

Do not ask the model. Read it.

Question + passage
Qwen1.5-7Bone forward pass per prompt, no text written
Score above a threshold?yes: refuse, no: answer
S1 · REFUSAL PROBABILITY

How likely is it that the reply starts with a refusal code?

S2 · YES OR NO

Ask "can this be answered from the passage?". Compare the chance of No with the chance of Yes.

S3 · LINEAR PROBE

A small classifier trained on the model's hidden state: the numbers inside it.

About 0.3 seconds per example on a T4. No judge, no API key, $0.

About 1.5 minutes. A probe is just a simple classifier (logistic regression) that looks at the model's internal numbers; "hidden state" means the numbers the model computes on the way to its answer. Higher score always means "refuse". S1 also counts the wrapped forms the model sometimes uses (for example "**REFUSE..."), about 30% of its coded refusals. S3 is trained with the benchmark's labels; S1 and S2 at their default thresholds use no labels. Say that out loud: it is why the probe is a diagnostic, not a fair like-for-like replacement for the baseline. Likely question: "Where is your novelty?" Reading these signals for grounded refusal on this benchmark, with a small open model, tested against the paper's baseline, plus the honest report of the version that did not work. Extension 2 · 2 of 4 · how we kept the test honest

Variants of one question never sit on both sides of the test

100 source questions are split into 5 groups. The threshold and the probe are tuned on 4 groups and tested on the fifth, five times.

tune
tune
tune
tune
test
tune
tune
test
tune
tune

...and so on for all five groups. Every example is scored by a rule that never saw its own question.

Why not a random split?

Variants of the same question would land in both parts, and the result would look better than it is.

Uncertainty

Intervals come from resampling whole questions, 2,000 times.

Rule fixed in advance

A rule is "better" only if its whole interval is above zero.

About 1.5 minutes. This is the part a careful committee member will like, and a tired one will skip: slow down for the first picture. Our unit test corrupts the labels of one group and checks that the threshold for that group does not change. Honest limit to state if asked: the intervals resample the held-out predictions; the fitting is not repeated inside each resample, so they do not capture variability of the fitted threshold or probe. The success rule was written down before the run: signal if the lower end of S1's AUROC interval is above 0.5; gain if the tuned threshold beats the default with its interval above zero. About 2 minutes. This is the "result quality" part of the rubric: read the verdict out loud, do not oversell. The signal column says all three readouts are above 0.5 (their 95% intervals start at 0.70 or higher). The last columns say what a rule would do in practice: thresholding S1 or S2 does not beat letting the model answer. The probe has the best number but its interval is -0.014 to +0.065. The probe was trained with the benchmark's labels and the baseline was not, so even a clear win would need that caveat. Hostile question: "Your baseline depends on an unvalidated Gemini judge for 64% of replies, while your rules are scored on labels." Answer: treat the comparison as provisional; I compare on the same 770 examples with question-clustered intervals, I claim no winner, and grading the rest plus the 30-reply judge check are the two next steps.

About 1.25 minutes. The overall balanced accuracy hides large trade-offs by type. Contradiction: the baseline misses 71%; the probe misses 19% and S2 23%, but they refuse more answerable questions (0.17 rises to 0.29 and 0.50). Ambiguity improves the same way. Conclusion

What we can say, what we cannot, and what a full run needs

We can say

  • A small model's scores and hidden states carry real information about when to refuse (AUROC 0.73 to 0.80).
  • Asking it to diagnose first backfires.

We cannot say

  • That our method beats the baseline.
  • Why the model answers "clear".
  • That this holds for other models.

A full run needs

  • All 1,560 replies graded.
  • The 30-reply check of the judge.
  • More seeds, models and benchmarks.

Central finding: a gap between decodable and deployable. The signal is there, but simple decision rules do not turn it into a clear gain.

About 1.5 minutes. End on the honest statement, then stop talking and invite questions. "A full run" is the course's term: we ran a smaller-scale version because of the free resources; here is exactly what the full version needs. Cost stays near $0 on the free tiers; it is time (judge quota) that limits us, not money. Related follow-up work to name if asked: Yin et al. (do models know what they do not know), R-Tuning (training a model to say "I don't know") and Self-RAG (a model that critiques its own retrieval). All three change the model by training; ours keeps the weights fixed and reads signals it already produces. In the 12-minute deck this is the end of slide 7.
