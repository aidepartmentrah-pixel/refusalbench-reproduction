Reading, Not Asking

Probing a small language model's internal signal for selective refusal in grounded question answering.

Abbass Zahreddine · M2 Data Science, Lebanese UniversityPaper: RefusalBench, EACL 2026
About 0.5 minutes. Say who you are and give the whole story in three sentences, so the room knows where it is going. "I took a research paper about when language models should refuse to answer. I reproduced it with a small model on a Google Colab T4. I tested two ideas to improve it. The first failed clearly. The second finds a real signal inside the model, but it does not clearly beat the baseline, and I will say exactly what the numbers do and do not prove." The paper · Problem

A grounded model must refuse when its evidence is flawed, and say why

A grounded model answers only from the passage it is given. Selective refusal means: answer when the passage supports it, refuse and name the reason when it does not.

About 1.25 minutes. Define the two terms out loud: grounded, and selective refusal. The example is Figure 1 of the paper: one sentence added to the passage turns an answerable question into one the model must refuse. The six reasons, with the benchmark's own examples: ambiguity ("the bat's weight" when the text has a baseball bat and an animal); contradiction ("revenue was $10M" and "revenue was $12M"); missing information ("who is the CEO?" when the text only describes products); false premise ("why did the Mars division fail?" when there is no Mars division); granularity mismatch ("average salary" when only two of fifty salaries are given); epistemic mismatch ("is this a good investment?" when only a stock price is given). Likely question: "Why not always answer?" A confident answer from a flawed passage can be wrong and harmful; the paper reports models refusing over 60% of answerable queries, or answering confidently despite critical information defects.

About 1.5 minutes. Click through the three steps. Step 1: the benchmark is generated, not collected. Several strong LLMs rewrite a good example to break it in a known way, and a broken example is kept only if every model approves it (the paper says this filters model-specific artifacts). An expert checked 180 random examples: 93.1% were valid. So every example has a known correct reply. Step 2: the bar shows the data: 536 can be answered (the mild versions), 1,064 must be refused. All 1,600 are variants of only 100 questions, so they are not independent: this is why our later tests keep variants of one question together. Step 3: three roles. Builders wrote the test (Claude, GPT-4o, DeepSeek-R1, Nova Pro; already done, we did not rerun it). The target model is the student. The judge is the grader: it must be a different model from the student, because a model rates its own answers too kindly. A refusal is correct only if its code equals the expected code (a text match); an answer is correct if the judge scores it 4 or 5. Likely question: "Who judges the judge?" In the paper an expert checked the benchmark examples, not the judge. For our Gemini judge the 30-reply hand check is still to do; it is listed as a limit.

About 1 minute. All numbers are the paper's own: 73.0% and 47.4% are in the introduction and Figure 5; 13.0% to 56.1% is the scaling section; "no model above 80% on both" is the Figure 5 caption. The Qwen-7B refusal accuracy of about 5% is read from a small plot, so say "about". The paper measures this weakness; it does not propose a way to fix it. That is where our work starts. Say the closing sentence out loud: the paper part is over. Then define the three terms once, because the numbers that follow use them. Worked example of the terms, if asked (invented numbers): 10 answerable and 10 must-refuse questions; the model refuses 2 answerable (false refusal 20%) and answers 4 that needed refusing (missed refusal 40%); of the 6 it refused only 1 gave the right reason (refusal accuracy 10%). Our summary, balanced accuracy, is 1 minus the average of the two error rates: 1 - (20% + 40%) / 2 = 70%, where 50% is a coin flip. We use it because two thirds of the examples must be refused.

About 1.5 minutes. This is the "code and reproduction" part of the rubric: be exact about what is the same and what changed. The diagram is the whole evaluation. A reply goes to the parser. If it is exactly one clean refusal code (528 of 1,560 replies, 34%), it is compared with the expected code directly, no judge. Everything else (1,032 replies, 66%: free text, an unknown code, or several codes) goes to the Gemini judge. Careful: a reply with no code is NOT assumed to be an answer, because a small model often refuses in plain words. Click once: for classmates who have never run a model on a GPU. A 7B model is about 15 GB at 16-bit, which barely fits a 15 GB T4 and leaves no room to work; storing each weight in 4 bits brings it to about 5 GB. Our code is cloned from GitHub in a Colab notebook, runs in batches, and saves every batch to Google Drive; if Colab disconnects, run it again and it resumes. Click again for what was kept and what changed. The prompt was sent as a single user message, as in the original code. Likely question: "Does 4-bit change the results?" It can, slightly. About 1 minute. Read the headline: what reproduces is the pattern, not the exact numbers. Refusal accuracy is low in both (our 0.114 against the paper's about 0.05, read from a plot). Our answer accuracy is higher (0.690 against 0.561); I do not claim to beat the paper, because the judge, the 4-bit model and the software differ. The graded part is a seeded random sample of the 1,560 generated replies, and our comparison script checks it is representative. 40 examples ran out of GPU memory in the first run and are excluded (2.5%). About 1.25 minutes. A probe is just a simple classifier (logistic regression) that looks at the model's internal numbers; the hidden state is the numbers the model computes on the way to its answer. Higher score always means "refuse". Nothing is generated: each score comes from one forward pass under its own prompt (S1 uses the paper's prompt, S2 a second yes/no prompt, S3 reads the hidden states of either pass). Say clearly: S1 and S2 at their default thresholds use no labels; S3 is trained with the benchmark's labels. That is why the probe is a diagnostic of what the hidden state contains, not an equal-training comparison with the baseline. Why the five groups: 1,600 examples are variants of 100 questions. A random split would put variants of the same question in training and test, and the result would look better than it is. A unit test corrupts the labels of one group and checks that the threshold for that group does not change. Honest limit: the intervals resample the held-out predictions; the fitting is not repeated inside each resample. Success rule: signal if the lower end of S1's AUROC interval is above 0.5; gain if the tuned threshold beats the default with its interval above zero. Scoring takes about 0.3 seconds per example on a T4. Likely question: "Where is your novelty?" Reading these signals for grounded refusal on this benchmark with a small open model, tested against the paper's baseline, with the version that did not work reported honestly. Related work (Yin et al., R-Tuning, Self-RAG) changes the model by training; ours keeps the weights fixed.
Extension 2 · Results

Extension 2 result: the signal exists, a better decision is not shown

{{SVG_RESULTS}}
Signal exists → deployment gain not established. The probe's interval crosses zero, so we claim no improvement.
About 1.5 minutes. This is the "result quality" part of the rubric: read the verdict out loud, do not oversell. Left (question A): all three readouts separate the cases far better than chance; their intervals start at 0.70 or higher. So the information is there. Right (question B): would using it make better decisions than the judged baseline (balanced accuracy 0.690)? S1's whole interval is below zero (slightly worse). S2 is unclear. The probe S3 has the best point estimate, +0.028, but its interval runs from -0.014 to +0.065, so we do not claim an improvement. With five comparisons and no correction, even S1's "worse" is not robust. Why a good AUROC does not give a better decision: AUROC measures ranking over every possible threshold; a deployed rule needs one operating point, and moving the threshold only trades one error for the other. In the trade-off picture the baseline sits close to the S1 and S2 curves and just above the probe's. Per failure type (same 770 examples, needed refusals missed / answerable refused): contradiction baseline .71 / .17, S2 .23 / .50, probe .19 / .29; ambiguity baseline .74 / .09, probe .45 / .23; false premise baseline .19 / .33, probe .25 / .23. So the probe finds many more contradictions and ambiguities, but refuses more answerable questions. Hostile question: "Your baseline depends on an unvalidated Gemini judge for most replies, while your rules are scored against labels." Answer: treat the comparison as provisional; I compare on the same 770 examples with intervals that resample whole questions, I claim no winner, and grading the rest plus the 30-reply judge check are the two next steps.
Conclusion and limitations
Decodable≠Deployable

The signal is inside the model. Turning it into a better decision is a separate problem.

We can say

  • A small model's scores and hidden states carry real information about when to refuse.
  • Asking it to diagnose first backfires.

We cannot say

  • That our method beats the baseline.
  • Why the model answers "clear".
  • That this holds for other models.

A full run needs

  • All 1,560 replies graded.
  • The 30-reply check of the judge.
  • More seeds, models and benchmarks.
About 0.75 minutes. End on the honest statement, then stop talking and invite questions. Central finding in one line: decodable is not deployable. A signal can be present in a model and still not be turned into a clearly better decision by simple rules. "A full run" is the course's term: we ran a smaller-scale version; here is exactly what the full version needs. Related follow-up work to name if asked: Yin et al. (do models know what they do not know), R-Tuning (training a model to say "I don't know"), Self-RAG (a model that critiques its own retrieval). All three change the model by training; ours keeps the weights fixed and reads signals it already produces.
